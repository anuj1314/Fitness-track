## Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

BMR is the energy your body needs at rest for essential functions such as
breathing, circulating blood, and maintaining body temperature. It excludes
exercise and everyday activity; it is not your daily eating target.

Using the Mifflin–St Jeor equation for men:

```text
BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
    = (10 × 78) + (6.25 × 175.3) − (5 × 28) + 5
    ≈ 1,740 kcal/day
```

| Activity level | Approximate maintenance calories |

| Lightly active: exercise 1–3 days/week | 2,390 kcal/day |
| Moderately active: exercise 3–5 days/week | 2,700 kcal/day |
| Very active: hard exercise most days | 3,000 kcal/day |

These activity categories are rough estimates. Your desk job reduces movement
during working hours, while running and gym sessions increase energy needs.
Running distance, workout intensity, daily steps, and recovery are still unknown;
exercise frequency alone cannot establish your maintenance calories. Avoid
adding exercise calories again if they are already included in the activity
estimate. Refine maintenance using average body weight and food intake over
2–3 weeks.

## Metabolism: fast versus slow

Metabolism is the collection of processes that use and transform energy in the
body. Daily energy expenditure includes resting needs, digestion, deliberate
exercise, and everyday movement such as walking, standing, and fidgeting.

People do differ, but “fast” and “slow” are broad descriptions, not fixed body
types. Body size, muscle mass, age, genetics, hormones, and movement all matter.
Muscle uses more energy at rest than fat, but gaining muscle does not create an
enormous increase in resting calorie burn.

Someone who appears to eat freely may move more or eat less overall than it
seems. Prolonged dieting can lower energy expenditure through weight loss,
less spontaneous movement, and metabolic adaptation. Difficulty gaining muscle
alone does not prove a fast metabolism.

For planning, use your observed weight trend rather than a metabolism label.
Unexplained weight changes with symptoms such as palpitations, unusual fatigue,
or heat/cold intolerance warrant a discussion with a clinician.

## Macronutrients, micronutrients, and fibre

Both macronutrients and micronutrients are parts of nutrition. The names refer
to the quantities needed, not their importance.

| Category | Examples | Main roles |
|---|---|---|
| Macronutrients | Protein, carbohydrates, fats | Energy, growth, repair, and body functions |
| Micronutrients | Vitamins and minerals | Bone health, oxygen transport, immunity, and energy metabolism |
| Dietary fibre | Fibre in legumes, oats, fruit, vegetables, and whole grains | Bowel regularity, gut bacteria, and other digestive and metabolic benefits |

**Fibre is a type of carbohydrate**, so it belongs within the macronutrient
category. Human digestive enzymes cannot fully break it down. Some passes
through the gut; some is fermented by gut bacteria. It is useful to track fibre
separately, alongside the main protein, carbohydrate, and fat targets. Nutrition
labels differ in whether their carbohydrate figure includes fibre; follow the
convention used by your food label or tracking app rather than adding it twice.

One food can provide several categories: dal contains protein, carbohydrates,
fibre, folate, and iron. Meeting calorie and macro targets alone does not ensure
adequate vitamins and minerals.

## Macros for building muscle

Muscle growth needs progressive resistance training, enough food, and recovery.
Macro targets support training; they do not build muscle on their own.

- **Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day, or approximately **125–172 g/day** at 78 kg.
  A practical target is **160 g/day**, with **150–170 g/day** as a useful working
  range, spread across your usual meals.
- **Fat:** roughly **20–35% of daily calories**. A practical starting target
  is **75–90 g/day** for your initial 2,900–3,100 kcal eating plan.
- **Carbohydrates:** use the calories remaining after protein and fat.
  They help fuel training; there is no single required amount for muscle growth.

Protein and carbohydrate each provide approximately 4 kcal/g; fat provides
9 kcal/g.

```text
Carbohydrate grams = [daily calories − (protein grams × 4) − (fat grams × 9)] / 4
```

## Starting muscle-gain targets

For gradual muscle gain, start around **150–250 kcal/day above your actual
maintenance**. The examples below use a 200 kcal surplus, 160 g protein,
and 80 g fat. These illustrate different activity assumptions; they are not
four separate plans to follow.

| Activity level | Daily calories | Protein | Fat | Carbohydrates |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sedentary | 2,290 kcal | 160 g | 80 g | 233 g |
| Lightly active | 2,590 kcal | 160 g | 80 g | 308 g |
| Moderately active | 2,900 kcal | 160 g | 80 g | 385 g |
| Very active | 3,200 kcal | 160 g | 80 g | 460 g |

Carbohydrates are rounded to the nearest gram. Given your desk job, three weekly
runs, and daily gym sessions, use **2,900–3,100 kcal/day as an initial trial**,
then calibrate it. This range does not establish that you are in a surplus.
At **3,000 kcal**, an internally consistent example is **160 g protein,
80 g fat, and 410 g carbohydrates**:

```text
(160 × 4) + (80 × 9) + (410 × 4) = 3,000 kcal
```

## Fibre and why vegetables appear in the plan

Aim for roughly **30–38 g fibre/day**. The adult male reference intake at your
age is 38 g/day; build toward it gradually according to tolerance. Increase
fibre slowly and drink enough fluids, especially when exercising in the heat.

- Fibre helps bowel regularity by holding water and/or adding bulk to stools.
- Some fibres feed gut bacteria that produce useful compounds.
- Certain fibres help moderate post-meal blood glucose and lower LDL cholesterol.
- Fibre can improve fullness. For muscle gain, excessive fullness may make it
  harder to eat enough; more fibre is not automatically better.

Vegetables also supply vitamins, minerals, and other plant compounds. You do
not need them at every meal: **a serving at lunch and dinner, plus fruit during
the day**, is a practical pattern. Rotate greens, beans, carrots, capsicum, and
gourds. Dal, rajma, chana, oats, whole-wheat chapatis, nuts, and fruit already
contribute fibre, so count the whole diet.

Keep large bean portions and high-fibre meals away from a run if they cause
bloating or urgency. Banana, bread, or rice may be easier to tolerate near
training.

## Micronutrients in a vegetarian diet

| Nutrient | Useful sources and practical considerations |
|---|---|
| Vitamin B12 | Dairy and reliably fortified foods; vegetarian diets can fall short. Discuss a suitable supplement if intake is inadequate. Unfortified plant foods are not reliable sources. |
| Iron | Dal, beans, soy, and leafy greens; eat with vitamin C sources such as lemon, guava, or capsicum to improve absorption. Avoid tea/coffee right with iron-rich meals. |
| Calcium | Milk, paneer, calcium-set tofu, and fortified alternatives; diluted buttermilk contributes a variable amount. |
| Vitamin D | Fortified foods and appropriate supplementation when indicated; living in a sunny city does not guarantee adequate status. |
| Zinc | Legumes, dairy, nuts, seeds, and whole grains. |
| Iodine | Use iodised salt in normal amounts; do not increase salt just to obtain iodine. |

Include nuts or seeds for unsaturated fats; ground flaxseed or chia and walnuts
provide the plant omega-3 fat ALA. Use labels to check fortification. Avoid
high-dose iron or vitamin D supplements without an appropriate indication.

## Adjusting the plan

- Weigh yourself under similar conditions and compare weekly averages.
- Aim for gradual gain of approximately **0.1–0.25% of body weight per week**
  (about **0.08–0.20 kg/week** at 78 kg).
- If weight is not increasing after 2–3 weeks, add 100–150 kcal/day.
  If gain is consistently faster than intended, reduce by a similar amount.
- Track strength and waist measurements alongside weight. Beginners can also
  gain muscle at maintenance calories; a surplus is not always necessary.
- Follow a consistent resistance-training program and get 7–9 hours of sleep.
- Keep protein similar on rest days. Adjust carbohydrates to training demands
  when useful. If recovery suffers, include easier training or rest days rather
  than assuming every gym session must be hard.
